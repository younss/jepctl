\begin{abstract}
\sloppy
This is an engineering report, not a research contribution. Joint-Embedding Predictive
Architectures (JEPAs) are an active research programme
\cite{lecun2022path,ijepa2023,vjepa2_2025,lewm2026,jepaanything2026}, and the
checkpoints and results they produce are published openly. Turning them into something a
practitioner can actually run --- against their own camera, on their own laptop, wired
into their own application --- is a separate, unglamorous amount of work that nobody had
done. \jepctl{} is that work: a single-binary local runtime, written in Rust with no
Python dependency, that pulls published JEPA-style checkpoints, reports exactly how much
of each one loaded, and serves embeddings over a REST and SSE API, a command line, and a
desktop testbench. On top of it I packaged four worked demonstrations, each built only
from operations the research already justifies: few-shot visual and audio recognition
with a per-patch divergence map; an online latent world model over the live camera that
predicts the next embedding and surfaces its prediction error as a surprise signal,
following the loop described in LeWorldModel \cite{lewm2026}; a robot arm driven by
posture mirroring or by minimising a latent distance to a goal image; and a multimodal
companion that fuses vision and audio locally. I trained nothing, invented no method, and
ran no comparative evaluation. What I report instead is what an integrator needs to know:
the checkpoint coverage obtained for six published encoders, the latencies and memory
footprints measured on one machine, one memory-retention defect that cost a factor of
seven in resident memory until it was found, and an explicit account of what this class
of model does not do. \jepctl{} is Apache-2.0 licensed and available at
\url{https://github.com/younss/jepctl}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A predictive model of the world is useful only if something can run it. The JEPA line of
work \cite{lecun2022path,ijepa2023,vjepa2_2025} argues that a system should learn by
predicting \emph{representations} of what it has not yet seen rather than reconstructing
pixels; recent work trains such a model stably end to end from raw pixels
\cite{lewm2026} and argues the principle carries far beyond vision
\cite{jepaanything2026}. The science is being done, and done in the open: the papers are
public, the checkpoints are downloadable.

What is missing is the last mile. A practitioner who wants to point I-JEPA or V-JEPA~2 at
their own webcam assembles a Python environment, writes their own preprocessing, guesses
at the checkpoint's tensor naming, and discovers only at inference time whether any of it
was right. Language models crossed that gap when single-binary runtimes turned ``pull a
model, run it, call an API'' into one line \cite{ollama}. Representation learning has had
no equivalent.

\jepctl{} is an attempt to build one. It is a single Rust binary with no Python
dependency that downloads and loads JEPA-style checkpoints with an explicit report of how
many tensors were covered, serves embeddings over HTTP, a live SSE stream, and a command
line, and ships a desktop testbench with four worked demonstrations.

\subsection{Scope: what this paper is, and what it is not}
\label{sec:scope}

I want to be precise about the genre, because it determines how the rest of this document
should be read.

\textbf{This paper is an engineering report.} Its subject is a tool: its design, the
decisions behind it, the defects found while building it, and measurements taken on one
machine. It is offered to practitioners and integrators who want to use JEPA encoders in
an application, and to researchers who would like their published checkpoints to be
easier for other people to run.

\textbf{It is not a research contribution.} I did not train a model, propose a method,
prove anything, or run a comparative evaluation against baselines. Every representation
used here comes from a checkpoint someone else published and trained. Where this report
describes a loop or a formula, it is describing \emph{what the software computes and
which published work it is taken from}, not a new result. In particular:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The predict-then-measure-surprise loop of Section~\ref{sec:world} is the one
  described by Maes et al.\ \cite{lewm2026}; my implementation deliberately weakens it
  (frozen encoder, small online predictor) so that it runs on a laptop and can be watched
  in seconds. That substitution makes it a demonstration, not a reproduction, and
  certainly not an improvement.
  \item The goal-reaching controller of Section~\ref{sec:robot} minimises a latent
  distance and plans by rolling out a fitted transition model, which is the structure used
  in \cite{lewm2026}. The fitting is deliberately simple; no claim is made about control
  performance.
  \item The design constraint that nothing above the encoder should assume a modality is
  taken from the argument in \cite{jepaanything2026}. I do not implement their method.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The contribution, such as it is, is availability.} Published research becomes
useful to a business when someone can run it in an afternoon, see what it does, and call
it from their own code. The numbers in Section~\ref{sec:measure} are engineering figures
--- latency, resident memory, checkpoint coverage --- reported so an integrator can plan,
not so a method can be ranked.

\section{What this tool builds on}

This section records which published results \jepctl{} depends on and what it takes from
each. It is not a survey, and it does not attempt to explain the theory better than the
original papers do; a reader who wants the science should read them.

\paragraph{The encoders I package.}
\jepctl{} runs checkpoints, and does not train them. The catalogued encoders are I-JEPA
\cite{ijepa2023}, V-JEPA~2 \cite{vjepa2_2025}, DINOv2 \cite{dinov2_2023}, plain ViT
\cite{vit2021}, and AudioMAE \cite{audiomae2022}. The practical property all of them
provide, and that every demonstration in this report relies on, is a patch-level
representation in which semantically similar content lands close together and nuisance
variation matters less than it does in pixel space. That property is theirs, not mine.

Two of those five are not joint-embedding predictive architectures and I do not want the
catalogue to blur the distinction. DINOv2 is trained by self-distillation with no explicit
predictor, and AudioMAE is a masked autoencoder that reconstructs spectrogram patches
through a decoder. They are catalogued because they expose the same interface and the same
usable property, a patch-level latent space that is stable under nuisance variation, not
because their training objective is a JEPA one. Where this report says ``JEPA-style
encoder'' it means that shared interface and property, and nothing about how the weights
were obtained.

\paragraph{LeWorldModel \cite{lewm2026}: where the World loop comes from.}
Maes et al.\ train an encoder and a predictor end to end from pixels with two loss terms:
a mean-squared next-embedding prediction loss, and SIGReg, a regulariser that projects
embeddings onto random directions and applies a normality test to each one-dimensional
projection so the latent distribution is pushed toward an isotropic Gaussian, which is
what keeps the training from collapsing. Two things in that paper are the reason
Section~\ref{sec:world} exists: the predictor is autoregressive in latent space, and the
resulting prediction error is a usable \emph{surprise} signal that flags implausible
events. I reuse the shape of that loop. I do not reuse the training: my encoder is frozen
and my predictor is a small online fit, which means none of their stability contribution
is being tested or reproduced here.

\paragraph{JEPA-Anything \cite{jepaanything2026}: a design constraint.}
Cui et al.\ argue the JEPA principle is domain-agnostic and validate a factorised
predictive design across seven domains. I implement none of it. What I took is an
architectural rule for the runtime: nothing above the encoder should assume a modality.
In \jepctl{} an image, a video clip, and an audio spectrogram are the same object --- an
input that becomes an embedding --- and the matcher, the world model, and the control
loops are written against embeddings rather than pixels.

\paragraph{Tooling precedent.}
The shape of the product is borrowed from local language-model runtimes \cite{ollama}:
one binary, a model catalogue, pull and serve, an HTTP API. Weights are read via
\code{safetensors} \cite{safetensors}; inference uses \code{candle} \cite{candle}.

\paragraph{What this class of model does not do.}
JEPA encoders are not generative: they do not decode a latent back to pixels. They do not
estimate metric depth. These are not limitations of my implementation but properties of
the models, and both the software's interface and Section~\ref{sec:limits} say so
explicitly, because a visualisation that implies otherwise would mislead a user who is
deciding whether the approach fits their problem.

\section{Architecture}

\jepctl{} is written in Rust and builds to one binary of roughly 14\,000 lines across
eleven top-level modules. Inference uses \code{candle} \cite{candle} with Metal, CUDA, or
multi-threaded CPU backends selected at runtime. The HTTP layer is \code{axum}; the
desktop testbench is a native window hosting a dependency-free web UI. This section
documents the structure, the runtime behaviour, and the interactions between components,
since those are the parts an integrator has to reason about. Figure~\ref{fig:overview}
gives the overall picture; Figure~\ref{fig:arch} the dataflow.

One departure from strict layering is worth naming: \code{types} references application
modules because several API data types embed domain structures (a robot telemetry
snapshot, a gesture score). It is a convenience that keeps the wire format in one file,
at the cost of a dependency edge pointing the wrong way. The remedy is known and not yet
applied: split out a \code{protocol} module holding only the wire data types and their
serialisation, and leave \code{types} to the primitives, which restores the acyclic order
without scattering the wire format.

All shared state is reached through a single \code{AppState} handle cloned into every
request: the engine, the model catalogue, the authentication manager, the camera and
microphone supervisors, the frame ring buffer, the gesture and sound stores, the region
of interest, the robot handle, the companion handle, and the scene predictor. Each is an
\code{Arc} over the appropriate lock: \code{RwLock} where readers dominate (ring buffer,
prototype stores), \code{Mutex} where every access mutates (robot core, scene predictor),
and \code{watch} channels to broadcast telemetry without contention.

\subsection{Strict checkpoint loading}
\label{sec:weights}

A partially loaded Vision Transformer \cite{vit2021} is a silent failure: the forward pass succeeds, the
output has the advertised dimension, and every downstream metric is meaningless. \jepctl{}
therefore maps every parameter of the constructed backbone against the checkpoint,
trying the naming conventions of Hugging Face ViT/I-JEPA, Hugging Face DINOv2, Hugging
Face V-JEPA~2, and timm-style fused QKV, and reshaping tensors whose element count matches
(for instance a \code{Conv3d} patch kernel feeding a linear tubelet embedding). It then
returns a coverage report. A model whose coverage is below 100\,\% is refused with HTTP
422 rather than served. Positional embeddings are adapted separately: the CLS row is
sliced or synthesised as required, and the patch grid is resampled bicubically when the
checkpoint resolution differs from the configured one.

Weights are read from \code{safetensors} \cite{safetensors}, which is parsed and
memory-mapped but never executed; this removes the arbitrary-code-execution surface of
pickle-based checkpoints. Table~\ref{tab:weights} lists the coverage obtained for the
catalogued encoders.

\subsection{Modality-agnostic input path}

Images are centre-cropped and resized to the model input with the normalisation declared
by the manifest (ImageNet or Inception statistics). Video clips are decoded natively for
GIF and animated WebP and through an external \code{ffmpeg} binary for MP4/WebM, then
uniformly sub-sampled to the clip length the model expects; image models embed each
sampled frame and average. Audio is down-mixed to mono, resampled to 16\,kHz, and turned
into a Kaldi-style log-mel filterbank of $1024 \times 128$ for the AudioMAE front end
\cite{audiomae2022}. Because that encoder's CLS token was never trained as a summary, the
manifest selects mean pooling; using CLS yields near-identical vectors for every input,
which is exactly the kind of silent failure the coverage report is designed to prevent.

\subsection{Deployment posture}

The daemon binds \code{127.0.0.1} by default and can be switched to all interfaces from a
settings toggle, in which case authentication is mandatory. Bearer tokens are stored as
SHA-256 digests and compared in constant time; no CORS headers are emitted by default, so
a web page open in the user's browser cannot read camera frames or embeddings. Decoders
are bounded (8192\,px per side, 256\,MB of decoder allocation, 512 frames or 256 million
pixels per animation) so that a decompression bomb is refused before it is allocated.

\section{Demonstration 1: few-shot inspection and recognition}
\label{sec:fewshot}

The first application registers a small number of reference embeddings per class and
classifies by similarity. Let $\{z_i^{(c)}\}$ be the $\ell_2$-normalised embeddings
captured for class $c$ and $p_c$ their normalised mean. For a query $z$, a naive matcher
scores $\cos(z, p_c)$. That score is dominated by whatever the classes share --- the room,
the lighting, the operator's torso. \jepctl{} therefore also computes a contrastive score
in the centroid-removed subspace: with $\bar p = \frac{1}{C}\sum_c p_c$, it scores
$\cos(z - \bar p,\; p_c - \bar p)$, and reports a blend
$s_c = 0.35\,\cos(z,p_c) + 0.65\,\cos(z-\bar p, p_c - \bar p)$, gated by a threshold and a
minimum lead over the runner-up. Removing the shared component before comparing prototypes is
standard practice in few-shot classification rather than anything devised here; what the
tool adds is that the blend, the threshold, and the margin are exposed in the interface
and in the exported bundle, so an integrator can see and reproduce the decision. A
dedicated \emph{neutral} class absorbs frames in which
nothing intentional is present, which is what prevents an empty scene from being forced
into the nearest class.

\paragraph{Localising the deviation.}
Because the encoder emits one vector per patch, the same machinery localises \emph{where}
a query departs from its prototype: the per-patch distance to the best-ranked class's
patch prototype, arranged on the $16\times16$ ViT grid, is a divergence map. In the
industrial-inspection scenario shipped with the tool --- a bottle, capped or not --- two
short captures are enough to separate the two states, and the divergence map concentrates
on the neck. Nothing was ever trained to look for a cap; the localisation is a consequence
of patch-level semantics. This is the property that makes the approach economically
interesting: the classical alternative requires a labelled dataset and a training run per
defect class.

\paragraph{Why this is worth a practitioner{\rq}s attention.}
Glass and plastic produce specular highlights that move with the operator and the ambient
light. Pixel differencing responds strongly to those high-frequency luminance changes; the
the patch embeddings are comparatively stable under them, because the pretraining
objective rewards representations that survive nuisance variation. This is reported as an
observed behaviour of the shipped scenario on one desk, under one set of lights. It is
not a robustness result: establishing that would take a dataset, a protocol, and a
baseline, none of which are part of this work.

\section{Demonstration 2: an online latent world model over the camera}
\label{sec:world}

The World module implements the LeWorldModel loop \cite{lewm2026} with one deliberate
substitution: the encoder is frozen (any catalogued JEPA encoder) and only the predictor
is fit, online, on the live stream. That substitution throws away the representation-learning part of \cite{lewm2026} ---
precisely the part their SIGReg term exists to protect --- in exchange for something a
user can watch behave in seconds on a laptop, with no training run and no checkpoint to
distribute. It is a demonstration of the idea, not an implementation of their method. Figure~\ref{fig:loop} shows the loop.

\paragraph{Predictor.}
The pooled embedding is projected onto a fixed random basis of dimension $K=48$; by the
Johnson--Lindenstrauss lemma \cite{jl1984} this preserves distances well enough for the
error statistic while keeping the online fit cheap and stable. In that space a
per-dimension first-order autoregression $x_{t+1} = a \odot x_t + c$ is fit by decayed
least squares (decay $0.97$), falling back to persistence until enough transitions have
accumulated. A small multilayer perceptron trained online by stochastic gradient descent
would be more expressive, and it was rejected for an engineering reason rather than a
modelling one: decayed least squares has a closed-form update per sample, no learning
rate, and no way to diverge partway through a session. The predictor runs unattended in a
user's application, on a stream whose statistics change whenever the camera moves, and a
component that can silently destabilise there is worse than one that can only ever
underfit.

\paragraph{Surprise.}
Let $e_t = \|\hat z_t - z_t\|_2$ be the error of the prediction made at $t-1$, and
$\bar e$ an exponentially weighted estimate of the recent typical error. The reported
surprise is $\mathrm{clip}\big((e_t/\bar e - 1)/1.5,\,0,\,1\big)$, so that a scene the
model tracks well sits near zero and a genuine deviation spikes. This is the
operationalisation of the surprise evaluation in \cite{lewm2026}: prediction error in
latent space, not in pixel space, is what flags the implausible event. Per patch, the same
comparison against a constant-velocity prediction $2z_t - z_{t-1}$ yields a spatial
surprise map, which the interface renders as a glow on the diverging region and which
drives a configurable alarm with a timestamped, spatially localised incident log.

\paragraph{Recognition and named states.}
A bounded memory of normalised pooled embeddings gives a recognition score (nearest
neighbour by cosine), and novel states are appended, so the system reports whether the
current view is familiar. Users may additionally save named reference states (``empty
desk'', ``full shelf''), after which the module reports which one it sees or flags the view
as unknown.

\paragraph{Rendering it without overclaiming.}
The reconstruction shown to the user maps the live frame onto a surface whose relief is
derived from the semantic distance of each patch to a background prototype (the mean of
the patches whose distance to the frame centroid is below the median). A smoothstep
transfer keeps the background flat and extrudes only the foreground, and the patch grid is
upsampled bicubically so silhouettes are smooth. The interface labels this as what it
is: the real frame given shape by what the encoder perceives. It is neither a generated
image nor a metric depth map.

\section{Demonstration 3: an arm driven from latent space}
\label{sec:robot}

The robot module drives a 6-DoF arm with a gripper through a hardware abstraction trait
with two implementations: an in-memory backend feeding a WebGL twin, and a serial backend
speaking a Feetech STS3215 register protocol. A safety supervisor enforces per-joint
limits, a 1.5\,rad/s velocity ramp at 30\,Hz, and an emergency stop that only an
administrator can reset; with the physical backend every command is held until explicitly
approved. Two control modes are latent-space operations.

\paragraph{Mirror: teleoperation without skeleton tracking.}
The operator demonstrates a handful of postures, each associated with an arm pose. At run
time the arm does not snap to the winning class; it moves to the softmax-weighted blend of
the demonstrated poses, with weights
$w_c \propto \exp\big((s_c - \max_j s_j)/\tau\big)$, $\tau = 0.06$, candidates more than
$0.20$ below the best receiving zero weight and the blend suppressed entirely below a
score of $0.35$. Raising an arm slowly therefore sweeps the joint targets continuously
through the demonstrated poses. Unlike pose-estimation pipelines, no joint of the human
body is ever localised: the comparison is between whole-posture embeddings.

\paragraph{Visual goal reaching: energy minimisation in latent space.}
Following the planning structure of \cite{lewm2026}, the arm captures a goal embedding
$z_\star$ from the camera and then minimises
\begin{equation}
E(z, z_\star) \;=\; \frac{\lVert z - z_\star \rVert_2}{\sqrt{d}} ,
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the embedding dimension. A latent transition model is fit online from
exploration: triples $(z, a, z')$ collected while the arm babbles are used to fit a
locally weighted ridge regression $\Delta z = W[a;1]$, refit after every observation. At
each step the controller samples 96 candidate actions, predicts their outcome \emph{inside
the learned model}, executes the one with the lowest predicted energy plus exploration
noise, observes the real result, and learns from it. Until enough transitions exist the
policy is random, and the telemetry reports which regime is active together with predicted
versus observed energy, so the user can judge the model rather than trust it. Convergence
is declared against a noise floor measured from two observations of a static scene, and a
plateau detector reports when a single viewpoint cannot disambiguate further.

Because the virtual arm is invisible to a real camera, the observation used in simulation
is the camera frame with the twin's kinematics rendered into it over a frozen background,
so that the only thing changing between observations is the arm itself. This is a
simulation convenience, and the interface says so; with the physical backend the observation is
the raw camera frame.

\section{Demonstration 4: local multimodal interaction}
\label{sec:companion}

The companion is a virtual character that fuses two frozen encoders locally. Motion
attention is computed from the centroid of the thresholded difference between consecutive
$32\times24$ grayscale thumbnails at 10\,Hz and steers the head; visual cues are matched
at roughly 3\,Hz against the gesture prototypes; audio cues are matched at roughly
1.4\,Hz from the last 1.5\,s of microphone audio, zero-padded into the AudioMAE window.
Recognised cues fire behaviours with a two-second cooldown, and postures mapped to body
poses are blended by the same softmax rule as the arm. A 30\,Hz control loop renders the
result. The point of this module is the absence of the usual machinery: there is no
transcription model, no keyword spotter, and no object detector --- a clap and a wave are
both nearest-prototype lookups over local embeddings.

\paragraph{A memory bug worth reporting.}
Every model was originally constructed from a parameter map whose tensors were
autograd-tracked variables, because that is the natural way to copy a checkpoint into a
pre-built architecture. Inference through such tensors records a backward graph, and that
graph keeps every intermediate activation of every layer alive until the output is
dropped. Instrumenting the Metal allocator showed GPU allocation climbing linearly by
roughly 0.5\,GB per transformer layer, reaching 13.5\,GB after 24 layers for a single
V-JEPA~2 clip; with concurrent requests the process reached 36\,GB and was killed.
Rebuilding each model on detached tensors after loading --- storage is shared, so nothing
is copied --- reduced the peak resident set for one clip forward to 1.98\,GB, of which
1.2\,GB is the weights themselves. Latency is a separate question and I cannot answer it:
every timing in Table~\ref{tab:measure} was taken after the fix, no before-and-after
comparison was recorded, and the tracked-variable build no longer exists to measure. The
graph nodes it allocated per layer were certainly not free, but I have no number for what
they cost and will not estimate one. It is recorded here because the failure mode is
invisible in a framework that assumes training: nothing is wrong except that inference is
paying for a gradient nobody will ever use.

\paragraph{What was checked, and what was not.}
The repository contains 76 Rust unit and integration tests, including in-memory HTTP tests
of every API surface, and 4 UI tests. What has \emph{not} been done is a numerical
comparison against the reference PyTorch implementations, because the project has no
Python dependency. Implementations follow the reference code structure, including
V-JEPA~2's tiled sine/cosine rotary tables with interleaved rotation and the Kaldi Povey
window for the audio front end, and determinism and discriminativeness are checked, but a
bit-level equivalence claim would be unsupported, so none is made. The gap is closable
without taking on a Python dependency, and this is the intended route: commit one fixed
synthetic input as a \code{safetensors} file, record the reference implementation's output
for it once, off-line, and ship both as a test fixture. That turns the open question into
a stated numerical tolerance that regressions can be measured against, which is what an
integrator actually needs, and it costs one file rather than a runtime dependency.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nothing here is validated as science.} The demonstrations show that the
  operations run and behave sensibly on one desk; they do not establish accuracy,
  robustness, or generality. Anyone deciding whether this family of models suits their
  problem should read the papers that trained them and run their own trial on their own
  data.
  \item \textbf{Not generative, no metric depth.} JEPA encoders do not decode latents to
  pixels and do not estimate distance. The 3D reconstruction in the World module is the
  real camera image given relief by semantic foreground separation; calling it depth would
  be wrong.
  \item \textbf{The online predictor is weak by construction.} A per-dimension AR(1) fit in
  a 48-dimensional random projection is adequate to expose the predict-then-measure-surprise
  loop and to detect deviations, but it is not the learned predictor of \cite{lewm2026} and
  makes no claim to their control performance.
  \item \textbf{Single viewpoint.} Goal reaching can plateau at a pose that is
  view-equivalent to the goal; the system reports the plateau rather than pretending to
  converge.
  \item \textbf{Few-shot means few-shot.} Prototypes interpolate between what was
  demonstrated and do not extrapolate; recognition degrades under large changes of
  viewpoint, scale, or illumination relative to the captures.
  \item \textbf{No published Audio-JEPA checkpoint.} The audio path is complete, but the
  verified audio encoder is AudioMAE \cite{audiomae2022}; a JEPA-trained audio encoder in a
  loadable format would be a manifest entry away.
  \item \textbf{The serial robot backend is frame-tested only.} The STS3215 protocol is
  implemented from the register map and unit-tested at the frame level; it has not been run
  against physical hardware by the authors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

The JEPA literature has moved quickly, from a training principle \cite{lecun2022path} to
stable end-to-end world models \cite{lewm2026} and a claim of domain generality
\cite{jepaanything2026}. That work is not mine and this report does not extend it.

What I found missing was smaller and more mundane: between a published checkpoint and a
practitioner with a laptop and a camera sits a day of environment wrangling, an
undocumented tensor-naming convention, and no way to tell whether the model that just
loaded is the one the authors trained. \jepctl{} removes that day. Pull a checkpoint, be
told exactly how much of it loaded, and then reach for recognition, anomaly detection, or
control as operations on embeddings, from a command line or an HTTP call.

The demonstrations it ships are deliberately ordinary --- inspect a bottle, mirror a
posture, reach a pictured goal, react to a clap --- and none of them is novel. That is the
point. Each one costs a handful of captures rather than a labelled dataset, runs entirely
on the machine in front of you, and can therefore be shown to someone who has to decide
whether this family of models is worth their time. Making research legible and runnable
is not a scientific result, but it is the step at which published work starts being used,
and somebody has to do it.
